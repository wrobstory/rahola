0What a wave is

Before the ship, the sea. One fact about deep water drives everything in this story: long waves outrun short ones.

A water wave is gravity arguing with inertia. Depress the surface; gravity pushes it back; the overshoot travels. In water deeper than a wavelength, the strength of that restoring tug depends on the wavelength itself, so each wavelength settles its argument at its own speed:

ω² = gk, so  c = g/k dispersion: long waves travel fast, short waves travel slow — the one law this page keeps collecting interest on

Dispersion has a consequence you can watch. Add two wave trains of nearly equal wavelength and they beat: packets of large crests separated by lulls. Now track a packet instead of a crest. The crests move at c, but the packet moves at dω/dk, and for ω² = gk the derivative gives exactly c/2. Crests are born at the back of a group, sweep forward through it, and die at the front:

Hold on to that red marker. Groups carry the sea's energy, they arrive as discrete events, and by §4 they will turn out to be the clock that decides the moment of capsize — the one clock no measurement aboard the ship can read.

1A ship is a ball in a well

The righting-arm curve is the wall of a valley, and the ship is a ball rolling in it. The picture is old; the point is what the valley's rim does when stability erodes.

Write roll as one degree of freedom. Scale the angle by the angle of vanishing stability φv (so x = ±1 is the cliff edge) and time by the natural frequency. The whole family of ships this project studies obeys one equation:

x″ + 2ζx′ + q·x′|x′| + κ(τ)(x − x³) = f(τ) the ship: inertia, damping, softening restoring · κ — how much stiffness is left · f — what the sea is doing right now

The restoring term x − x³ is the archetype of every GZ curve that ends: it rises, peaks (at x = 1/√3 — the GZ-max of this hull), and dies at the escape angle. Integrate it and you get the valley — with a rim of finite height. The rim is the saddle: past it, the restoring moment pushes the ship over, not back.

2The sea is a random sum of cosines

An irregular seaway is a crowd of regular waves with random timing. This is physics, not convenience: to first order the surface equations are linear, so wave trains pass through one another unchanged, and any storm decomposes into independent components.

All a storm can choose is how its energy spreads across frequency. That choice is the spectrum, and for wind seas the JONSWAP field program measured it: energy piles up near a peak frequency set by wind and fetch, with a sharp low-frequency front and a power-law tail. The peakedness γ measures how narrowly the energy crowds the peak, and §0 tells you what narrowness buys: components of nearly equal wavelength beat, so a peaked spectrum makes a groupy sea. Pick amplitudes from the spectrum, give every component an independent random phase, and add:

f(τ) = Σj Aj cos(ωjτ + θj) amplitudes from the spectrum's energy; phases from a seeded random draw — the seed is the sea state's identity

One step remains between the sea and the ship: how a wave becomes a roll moment. In beam seas a hull tries to sit perpendicular to the local water surface, so to first order the forcing follows the wave slope, scaled by an effective wave-slope coefficient that stands in for hull geometry. And the ship answers selectively. Roll is the lightly damped motion — at the damping used here the resonant response is roughly ten times the static one — so the ship amplifies exactly the frequencies near its natural period and shrugs off the rest. That is why roll, alone among the six motions, owns this problem.

4Two clocks: vulnerability and encounter

The project's central finding fits in one sentence: capsize runs on two clocks, and motion history can read only one of them.

The slow clock is the well itself — κ eroding as cargo shifts, ice accretes, or a damaged compartment floods. It sets whether the ship is in danger and roughly when danger becomes acute. The fast clock is the arrival of a particular wave group. It sets the instant. 5From motion to alarm — the geometry of a margin

A warning score is a function of what you can measure: roll and roll rate. The best zero-training score is pure geometry — how fast is the ship allowed to be rolling, this far from the rim?

Near the saddle, the boundary between "swings back" and "goes over" is a line in the phase plane: the saddle's stable manifold. The split-time school (Belenky and colleagues) wrote its slope down for the piecewise-linear well, and the answer is the project's physics baseline — the danger margin:

x′crit(x) = [ζ + k₁κ + ζ²] (1 − |x|) the fastest outward roll rate the well can still forgive, at this heel — measured state above the line means the current encounter is already terminal

Read the three big tiles together and the two-clock picture becomes quantitative. Vulnerability is readable: a small CNN ranks danger windows far above chance, at every bandwidth, and no baseline matches its pooled operating cost. Encounter is not: once the regime is established, predicting which roll becomes the last one is coin-flip territory for every motion-only method — learned, classical, or physical. And the alarms a warning system does raise are mostly honest: three quarters or more of "false" episodes flagged a real critical wave group that the ship happened to survive. An outcome-based false-positive rate charges the detector for the sea's mercy.

Two conclusions did not survive: no detector's ≥90%-sensitivity threshold transferred across held-out failure families (D2 — the CNN included), and the bandwidth experiment's required operating-cost margin missed its predeclared 10% bar (8.6% — inconclusive, and reported as such). Ranking skill and a deployable threshold are different assets; this project kept ending up rich in the first and poor in the second.

8The leakage lesson — the audit's red pen

The first version of these results was better. It was also wrong, in a way almost every published paper in this field is wrong.

The original harness swept thresholds and reported, for each detector, "the lowest false-alarm rate at ≥90% sensitivity on the test set." That sentence contains the bug: the threshold was chosen using test outcomes, then graded on the same outcomes. With rare events, the lucky threshold always exists. The corrected protocol chooses on calibration data, freezes, and spends the test set once. The same detector, on the same data:

9Where the information ends — and what's next

The last experiment asked the sharpest question: is the CNN's ceiling an architecture problem or an information problem?

Because the simulator's truth is knowable, the project could restart thousands of futures from the exact hidden state — perfect knowledge of the ship, no knowledge of the coming waves — and compare everything on one balanced window set:

0.851
AUC restarting from the exact hidden state, futures independent — perfect ship-knowledge, no wave-knowledge
0.762
XGBoost on engineered features — the roll period is a stiffness gauge, and boring tools read it well
0.627
the CNN on the same balanced windows — much of its natural-population skill was prevalence structure

Two honest caveats made this comparison humbler and more interesting than planned. These are restart comparators, not ceilings: the sea has memory (§2's groups), so real motion history carries a preview of the next few waves that an independent-future restart deliberately throws away. And the filtered-state comparator underperformed for a mundane reason — its particle filter was weaker than a roll-period feature. What remains is still directional: there is real headroom above the CNN, it lives in state inference, and the cheapest tools claimed it first.

The forward path follows the information, not the architecture: the fast clock is invisible from onboard motion because the deciding waves have not arrived yet. The funded next hypothesis puts one cheap sensor upwave — a rangefinder's single spot, or a scout vessel's motion — and asks whether one advanced scalar is enough. Either answer closes the question this project was built to ask.
